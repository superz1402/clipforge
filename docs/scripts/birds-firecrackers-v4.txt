Have you ever noticed what happens when a firecracker suddenly explodes?
Within seconds, birds sitting peacefully on trees can take off in every direction.
But why? Are they simply scared of loud noises, or is something much deeper happening inside their bodies?
Well, there's some fascinating biology behind it.
Birds have highly developed sensory systems that help them detect potential danger in their environment.
When a firecracker explodes, the sudden, intense sound acts as an unexpected threat signal.
Their brains rapidly process this disturbance, activating something called the fight-or-flight response.
Their sympathetic nervous system kicks in.
Stress hormones increase, their muscles prepare for action, and their bodies become ready to escape.
And here's something even more interesting.
Birds don't understand that fireworks are meant for human entertainment.
To them, a sudden explosion could signal a predator, an environmental threat, or something dangerous nearby.
And sometimes, one bird taking flight can trigger hundreds of others to follow.
This is known as collective escape behaviour.
One bird reacts, others notice its sudden movement, and within seconds, an entire flock can take off together.
But the consequences aren't always temporary.
Sudden nighttime flights can cause birds to become disoriented, collide with buildings, abandon resting places, and waste valuable energy.
For a bird, that one-second explosion can become a much bigger survival challenge.
We might see fireworks as entertainment, but wildlife experiences them very differently.
